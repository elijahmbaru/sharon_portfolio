The goal

Let merchants accept PayPal on Paystack, without losing the trust of their customers.

PayPal wanted to reach more African merchants, and chose to do it by partnering with Paystack rather than building its own presence market by market. Together, we set out to offer PayPal as a payment option on Paystack checkout. I designed the end-to-end experience: how a merchant enables PayPal and gets verified, how a customer pays with it, and how merchants handle PayPal chargebacks from their Paystack dashboard.

Objective 01

Make merchant onboarding intuitive, even though it spans two companies’ systems.

Objective 02

Keep customers’ trust at checkout, especially when they’re sent to PayPal to sign in.

Why PayPal

What it offers merchants and their customers

Better success rates

Funds come from PayPal rather than the issuing bank, so fewer payments are blocked.

More ways to pay

Customers can pay by card or with their PayPal balance.

Trust and protection

A recognised brand, with buyer protection for customers and chargeback support for businesses.

01 · Merchant onboarding

Enabling PayPal, across two ecosystems

Educate first

Answer merchants’ questions before they ask them

When a merchant turns on PayPal for the first time, a short series of modals explains what it is, why it’s worth enabling, and what happens next. The content came from the questions merchants raised when we tested early prototypes with them.

Business account and verification

Make the hand-off to PayPal, and the wait, feel clear

Merchants need a PayPal business account. They can link one, create one, or upgrade a personal account, and they finish that on PayPal, including KYC, because Paystack doesn’t pass merchant details across. Back on Paystack, the dashboard shows exactly where verification stands. Through the partnership, verification takes days rather than the usual six to eight weeks.

Customer checkout

A familiar PayPal sign-in, framed by Paystack

Once PayPal is enabled, customers choose it on checkout and sign in to their PayPal account to pay.

Every hand-off between Paystack and PayPal should feel expected, never surprising.

02 · Refunds and chargebacks

PayPal disputes, handled on the Paystack dashboard

The claims process

How a PayPal dispute moves

A customer raises a dispute with their bank or with PayPal. It lands on the merchant’s dashboard, and the merchant has 48 hours to respond. Some actions close the case straight away. Others wait on the customer, who can accept or escalate. Once a case is escalated, PayPal reviews it and decides the outcome.

The constraint

PayPal sets the rules, so the design had to guide merchants through them

PayPal disputes work differently from other disputes on Paystack, and there was little room to change the process. The goal was to help merchants succeed within it, through clear actions, rules and education.

Accept

Issue a full refund to the customer.

Make an offer

A partial refund, a return for refund, or a replacement, each with an amount and a reason.

Add tracking

Allowed once, and only before any other action is taken.

Escalate to PayPal

Hands the case to PayPal. Every other action, including chat, is then closed.

Accept

Say what a refund costs before it happens

Accepting is the one action that can’t be undone, so the panel states the amount and that it comes out of the next payout. It also points to Make offer for merchants who want to settle for less. Once accepted, the status changes and the timeline records the refund.

Tracking information

A one-time action, flagged before it’s used

PayPal accepts tracking details only once, and not after an offer has been made. The form warns merchants up front, and asks for a tracking link only when there is one. After submitting, the details appear on the timeline and the tab explains why it’s locked. Any action other than accepting moves the dispute to In Progress.

Escalate to PayPal

Spell out what escalating gives up

Escalating hands the case to PayPal, and the merchant loses the chance to talk to the customer. Before they confirm, the panel lists what changes: chat closes, open offers are cancelled, PayPal reviews what was shared, and a decision can take up to 30 days. Afterwards, every tab shows that the issue is escalated and who escalated it.

Visibility for the team

Merchants and customers talk directly. Ops can see it all.

For PayPal disputes, merchants can chat with customers. The conversation, shared documents and history are logged on Watchtower for the operations team, with the resolve action turned off, since PayPal owns the outcome.

Emails

Bring merchants back at the moments that matter

Merchants don’t sit on the dashboard waiting for disputes. An email goes out when a dispute is raised, with the 48 hour deadline and a link to respond. When a customer escalates, a second email tells the merchant that PayPal has the case and nothing more is needed from them.

03 · Testing

Testing prototypes with merchants

Usability sessions

Two tasks, talked through out loud

Merchants worked through clickable prototypes while thinking aloud, and we followed up on the moments most likely to cause doubt. We ran a separate round on the disputes flow to find where merchants might get stuck before launch.

Task 1 · Enable PayPal

  • Do the educational modals answer the right questions?
  • Does the hand-off to PayPal make sense?
  • Is the verification checklist clear, and what else is expected there?

Task 2 · Pay with PayPal

  • Do customers trust being sent to PayPal to sign in?
  • Does anything unexpected happen between screens?

04 · Status

Ready to launch, once the partnership is

Where it stands

The designs are complete and tested with merchants. Launch is on hold while a few dependencies on the PayPal partnership are worked through. The experience will go live as soon as they’re resolved.
